Uniform integrability

  • Some results similar to those above can be obtained under a weaker condition called uniform integrability. E.g. see Theorem 2.5.10 on page 68 of A&L.

  • Tails of integrable functions: Show that \(f\in L^1(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)\) implies \(\lim_{t\to \infty} \int_{\{|f| > t\}}|f|d\mu = 0\).

    This result leads to the definition of uniform integrability.

  • Uniform integrability of a family of functions: Let \(\{f_\alpha, \alpha \in I\}\) be an arbitrarily indexed family of measurable functions on a measure space \((\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)\). The family \(\{f_\alpha, \alpha \in I\}\) is uniformly integrable if \[ \lim_{t \to \infty} \sup_{\alpha \in I} \int_{\{|f_\alpha| > t\}}|f_\alpha| d\mu = 0. \]

    See Definition 2.5.8 on page 65 of A&L, the section “uniform integrability” beginning on page 216 of Billingsley, or Section 6.5.1 of Resnick beginning on page 182. The definition is represented next for a sequence of random variables.

  • Uniform integrability of a sequence of random variables: Let \(\{X_n\}_{n\geq 1}\) be a sequence of random variables on a probability space \((\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)\). Then the sequence \(\{X_n\}_{n \geq 1}\) is uniformly integrable if for every \(\epsilon > 0\) there exists \(M_\epsilon > 0\) such that \[ \sup_{n \geq 1} \mathbb{E}[|X_n| 1(|X_n| > M_\epsilon)] < \epsilon. \]

  • Convergence result for uniformly integrable sequences of functions: Let \(\{f_n\}_{n\geq 1}\) be a sequence of integrable functions on a finite measure space \((\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)\) such that \(f_n \stackrel{\operatorname{a.e.}}{\longrightarrow}f\), where \(f\) is measurable. If the \(\{f_n\}_{n\geq 1}\) is uniformly integrable then

    1. \(f\) is integrable.
    2. \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \int |f_n - f|d\mu = 0\).

    See Theorem 2.5.10 on page 68 of A&L.

Differentiation

  • Absolute continuity and mutual singularity: Let \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) be measures on a measurable space \((\Omega,\mathcal{F})\).

    • If \(\mu(A) = 0\) implies \(\nu(A) = 0\) for all \(A \in \mathcal{F}\) then \(\nu\) is absolutely continuous (a.c.) with respect to \(\mu\) or dominated by \(\mu\). We write \(\mu \ll \nu\).
    • If there exists a set \(B \in \mathcal{F}\) such that \(\mu(B) = 0\) and \(\nu(B^c) = 0\) then \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are mutually singular. We write \(\mu \perp \nu\). Here the relation is symmetric, i.e. \(\mu \perp \nu \iff \nu \perp \mu\). See Example 4.1.4 on page 114 of A&L.

    See Definitions 4.1.1 and 4.1.2 on pages 113 and 114 of A&L.

  • Make a new measure by integrating a nonnegative function: Let \(f\) be a nonnegative measurable function on a measure space \((\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)\). Show that the set function defined as \[ \nu(A) \equiv \int_A f d\mu \] for all \(A\in \mathcal{F}\) is a measure on \(\mathcal{F}\).

    See Corollary 2.3.6 on page 53 of A&L. Note that \(\nu \ll \mu\), i.e. \(\nu\) is absolutely continuous with respect to \(\mu\).

  • Radon-Nikodym Theorem: Let \((\Omega,\mathcal{F})\) be a measure space and let \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) be \(\sigma\)-finite measures on \(\mathcal{F}\) such that \(\nu \ll \mu\). Then there exists a nonnegative measurable function \(f\) such that \(\nu(A) = \int_A f d\mu\) for all \(A \in \mathcal{F}\). Moreover, for two such functions \(f\) and \(g\) it holds that \(f = g\) almost everywhere.

    See Theorem 32.2 of Billingsley. The function \(f\) is called the Radon-Nikodym derivative of \(\nu\) with respect to \(\mu\). We may also call \(f\) the density of \(\nu\) with respect to \(\mu\). Often let \(\mu\) be the Lebesgue measure and set \(f\) to be a probability density function. Or set \(\mu\) to be the counting measure and \(f\) to be a probability mass function.

  • Relation between Riemann and Lebesgue integrals: Let \(f\) be a bounded measurable function on \(([a,b],\mathcal{B}([a,b]),\mu_L)\), where \(\mu_L\) is the Lebesgue measure. Then \(f\) is Riemann integrable on \([a,b]\) if and only if it is continuous almost everywhere (with respect to \(\mu_L\)). In this case the integral \(\int f d\mu_L\) is equal to the Riemann integral \(\int_a^b f(x)dx\).

    See Theorem 2.4.1 on page 61 of A&L. Compare to Theorem 5.6.1 on page 139 of Resnick and Section 17 of Billingsley beginning on page 221.

  • Example: Consider the measure space \(([0,1],\mathcal{B}([0,1]),\mu_L)\), where \(\mu_L\) is the Lebesgue measure, and let \(f(\omega) = 6 \omega(1-\omega)\) for \(\omega \in [0,1]\). Now define \(P(A) = \int_A f d\mu_L\).

    • Give an expression for \(P((a,b))\) for any interval \((a,b) \subset [0,1]\).
    • Show that \(P\) is a probability measure.
    • Let \(X(\omega) = \omega\) be a random variable on the probablity space \(([0,1],\mathcal{B}([0,1]),P)\). Give \(\mathbb{E}X\).

  • Example: Consider the measure space \((\{0,1,\dots,N\},\mathcal{P}(\{0,1,\dots,N\}),\mu)\), where \(\mu(A) = |A|\) for all \(A \subset \{0,1,\dots,N\}\) is the counting measure, and let \(p(\omega) = (1/2)^N {N\choose\omega}\) for \(\omega \in \{0,1,\dots,N\}\). Now define \(P(A) = \int_A p d\mu\) for each \(A \subset \{0,1,\dots,N\}\).

    • Give an expression for \(P(A)\) for any \(A \subset \{0,1,\dots,N\}\)
    • Show that \(P\) is a probability measure.
    • Let \(X(\omega) = \omega\) be a random variable on the probability space \((\{0,1,\dots,N\},\mathcal{P}(\{0,1,\dots,N\}),P)\). Give \(\mathbb{E}X\).
